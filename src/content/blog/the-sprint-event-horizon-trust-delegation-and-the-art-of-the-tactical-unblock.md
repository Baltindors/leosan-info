Once the development team crosses that threshold, the laws of physics change, time dilation kicks in, and as a Product Manager, your ability to alter their trajectory should be fundamentally restricted.

If you are constantly hovering over your engineering team mid-sprint, asking for status updates on a Vue component or trying to sneak "just one little feature" into the Express backend, you aren't managing a product—you're just micro-managing a speedrunner. And doing that usually ends with someone getting thrown into a pit of spikes.

### The Work Happens _Before_ the Music Changes

Think of sprint execution like a boss fight in a classic 90s RPG. By the time the screen flashes and the boss music starts playing, your job is already done. You should have already equipped the party, mapped out the dungeon, and explained exactly _why_ this particular boss needs to be defeated to save the user's workflow.

Your heaviest lifting—strategic alignment, backlog grooming, defining the ROI, and writing crystal-clear acceptance criteria—must happen before the sprint planning meeting concludes. If you've done your job correctly, the team should be entirely self-sufficient. Agile thrives on empowerment. You have to trust that the engineers know how to write the code and the designers know how to build the interface. Let them work. While they are executing in the present, your eyes should be firmly locked on the future, preparing the roadmap for the next quarter.

### The Red Telephone Philosophy

Staying out of the way does not mean going AFK. Here is the paradox of the PM during a release cycle: You must be entirely hands-off regarding their process, but instantly available to unblock their progress.

Call it the "Red Telephone" philosophy. If the team is grinding through a sprint and they encounter a logic gap in a user story, an edge case nobody predicted, or a technical trade-off that impacts the user experience, they are going to pick up that phone. When it rings, you drop everything.

You are the tactical unblocker. You don't need to know how to fix the server deployment, but you _do_ need to make the split-second business decision on whether to launch with a known minor bug or delay the release by two days to fix it. Your availability ensures that the team doesn't lose momentum debating business logic. You swoop in, provide the clarification, clarify the priority, and then immediately get out of their way so they can get back to their flow state.

### TL;DR

Your job as a PM is to prepare the team before the sprint starts, not to micromanage them while it's running. Trust your engineers and stay out of their way so you can focus on the future roadmap. However, the moment they hit a roadblock and need clarification, you must drop everything to unblock them. Be invisible until you are indispensable.
